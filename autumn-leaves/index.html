Why are leaves sometimes red and falling?

A few weeks of autumn colour hide three separate questions. How does a leaf change colour and come off the tree? What does the tree gain from doing it? And why do some parts of the world turn red while others go quietly yellow? This hour takes them in that order, and finishes with the Ice Ages.

How this hour works

Three questions, one leaf

How it works, what it is for, and why here but not there. Every section comes back to the leaves you brought.

Quick reviews

Where you see Quick review, there is a one-minute refresher on the physiology you need. Skim it if you know it; read it if you don't.

Predict before you look

Most results are hidden behind a reveal. Commit to a prediction first, out loud or in writing.

Bring your essay

Where you see From your essay, find the paragraph where you made that claim, or should have, and be ready to defend or change it.

The interactive models are deliberately simple toys, built to show an idea, not real numbers. Sections marked If time are extras we may skip.

By the end you should be able to

  • Describe the steps from an autumn cue to a fallen leaf, and say which steps can happen without the others.
  • Explain why yellow needs little explaining but red needs a lot.
  • Weigh the evidence that red protects the leaf from light and that it is a message to insects, and explain how recycling nitrogen links the two.
  • Suggest why red autumn trees are commoner in eastern North America and East Asia than in Europe.

No preparation needed

Everything you need is on these pages. Bring your essay and the leaves you collected.

Minutes 00–06

1 · Your leaves: where colour comes from

First minuteWrite your answer to the essay question in one sentence. Keep it. We come back to it at the end.
TogetherLay your leaves on the table from greenest to reddest. For each one: which species, where did you find it, and is it yellow, orange, red or brown? Did any species give you more than one colour?
Quick review · why a leaf has a colour

White light contains every colour. A pigment absorbs some wavelengths and lets the rest bounce back. We see the light that bounces back, not the light that is absorbed.

  • Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red, so green bounces back.
  • Carotenoids (yellow and orange) absorb blue. They sit alongside chlorophyll in the chloroplast all summer, hidden by it.
  • Anthocyanins (red and purple) absorb green. They are dissolved in the vacuole, not in the chloroplast.
  • Browns come from tannins and other compounds that oxidise as cells die.

Mix a leaf

Set each pigment and watch the leaf colour and the light it reflects. The bar on the right is a rough guess at how attractive that colour looks to an aphid; we need it in section 3.

Discuss
  1. Start from Summer and slide chlorophyll to zero without touching anything else. What colour do you get, and did the leaf have to make anything new?
  2. Now make the yellow leaf red. Which slider did you need? So which colour change costs the tree something?
  3. Copper beech has anthocyanin and chlorophyll. Why does it look dark rather than red? What does that tell you about what must happen to chlorophyll before an autumn leaf can look red?
Quick review · how a leaf makes anthocyanin

Anthocyanins are flavonoids, built by a chain of enzymes that starts from the amino acid phenylalanine and from sugars. Each arrow is one enzyme; you don't need to remember their names, only the shape of the pathway.

  • Raw materials: phenylalanine and sugar-derived building blocks. Senescing leaves have plenty of sugar, because they have stopped exporting much of it.
  • The switch: a group of three transcription factors (MYB, bHLH and WD40 proteins) turns the later enzymes on together. Light, cold and high sugar all switch it on.
  • Side branches: the same pathway also makes flavonols (colourless, absorb UV) and tannins (defence, and the browns of dead leaves).

Minutes 06–18

2 · Mechanism: from cue to fall

Opening questionA leaf goes yellow, then red, then falls. Is that one programme, or three that happen to run one after another? What would you look for to tell?
Quick review · how a tree turns a cue into a response

Every step in autumn follows the same chain you know from other signalling: something in the environment is detected, a signal is passed on, genes are switched on or off, and the new set of enzymes changes the cell.

Stage A · 4 min · What starts it?
Predict first Mature aspen trees in northern Sweden start changing colour on almost the same date every year. What is the trigger?
Reveal: three studies of the same tree species
What they didWhat they concluded
Followed one tree through one autumn (Keskitalo et al. 2005)Ageing started on 11 September, apparently set off by day length alone, then ran steadily whatever the weather.
Watched wild trees, and grew cloned trees under different day lengths (Fracheboud et al. 2009)Shortening days are the main trigger, but trees in the same place started on very different dates, and cold sped things up once they had started.
Grew 116 genetically different trees from across Sweden side by side in two common gardens, one in the north and one in the south (Michelson et al. 2018)The timing did not fit day length alone. The trigger must be something else to do with light.

Across many species, temperature matters too: warm autumns generally delay leaf colour (Gill et al. 2015). Whether a productive summer brings autumn forward is still argued about (Zani et al. 2020; Lu & Keenan 2022).

Common garden: plants from different places grown side by side in one site. Because they share an environment, differences between them must be genetic.

From your essaySeveral essays said ageing is triggered "solely" by day length. Which study supports that, and why should you be careful about it? Hint: with one tree in one year, what else changed on the same dates as day length?
Stage B · 4 min · A timetable for one leaf
Discuss
  1. Halfway through, the leaf powers the export of nutrients by respiration (in mitochondria), not photosynthesis. Why?
  2. Export stops around 29 September but the last leaves fall a week later. What would the tree lose if a leaf fell on 20 September instead?
  3. About 80% of the leaf's nitrogen and phosphorus is moved out. Most leaf nitrogen is not in chlorophyll. Where is it?
Where the nitrogen is

Most of it is in protein, above all Rubisco, the enzyme that fixes CO₂, and the proteins that hold chlorophyll in place. These are broken down to amino acids and sent out in the phloem. Chlorophyll itself is broken down mainly because loose chlorophyll is dangerous in the light; its breakdown products stay in the leaf (Hörtensteiner 2006).

Stage C · 4 min · How a leaf lets go
Quick review · the abscission zone
  • Where: a band of small, densely packed cells at the base of the leaf stalk (petiole). It forms when the leaf is young and waits.
  • The brake: a healthy leaf sends auxin down the stalk, which keeps the zone insensitive.
  • The trigger: as the leaf ages, the auxin supply falls and the zone becomes sensitive to ethylene.
  • The cut: ethylene switches on enzymes (cellulases and polygalacturonases) that digest the pectin "glue" between cell walls (the middle lamella). The leaf hangs by its veins until wind or its own weight snaps it.
  • The seal: a corky protective layer forms on the stem side, leaving a leaf scar that keeps water in and pathogens out.
Discuss
  1. Young oaks and beech hedges keep their dead brown leaves all winter (this is called marcescence). Are ageing and falling one programme or two?
  2. In one experiment, mutant plants that could not make anthocyanin dropped their leaves while still green when kept in bright, cold conditions (Hoch et al. 2003). What might that say about a link between colour and falling?
From your essayMost essays gave falling a sentence or two at the end. If you had 300 more words, what would they say?

Minutes 18–32

3 · Function: what is red for?

Opening questionYellow is mostly what is left behind. Red is made, days before the leaf is thrown away. Why pay for a pigment at the end of a leaf's life?
Stage A · 2 min · Two big ideas

Sunscreen (photoprotection)

Anthocyanin shades the ageing leaf from too much light, so it can keep working long enough to send its nutrients back to the tree.

A message to insects (coevolution)

Aphids arrive in autumn to lay eggs on trees. Red tells them "this tree is well defended, go elsewhere", or hides the yellow they find attractive.

Honest signal: a signal that is hard to fake because it is costly, so only a tree that can afford it can show it. Coevolution: two species each evolving in response to the other.

TogetherFor each idea: who benefits, the tree or the insect? And which trees should be the reddest: the healthiest, best-defended ones, or the most stressed, nutrient-poor ones?
My reading

Sunscreen: the tree benefits; the reddest trees should be the ones that need to recover nutrients most and face most light stress, such as nutrient-poor trees on cold, bright days. Message to insects: both benefit; if red is an honest signal, the reddest should be the best-defended, healthiest trees. So the two ideas make opposite predictions about nutrient-poor trees. A study of sugar maples found that the trees lowest in nitrogen went red earlier and more fully (Schaberg et al. 2003). Which idea does that favour?

Stage B · 3 min · Too much light for a dying leaf
Quick review · why too much light is harmful

Absorbed light is energy. A leaf can use it for photosynthesis, or shed it safely as heat. If it absorbs more than it can do either with, the extra energy is passed to oxygen and makes reactive oxygen species (such as hydrogen peroxide) that damage proteins and membranes. Cold makes this worse, because the enzymes of photosynthesis slow down while the sunlight does not. An ageing leaf is also dismantling the machinery that uses and sheds light.

In this toy model, move the sliders and watch what happens to the light a leaf absorbs.

Discuss
  1. Set a summer leaf (all chlorophyll, 20 °C). Is there harmful excess? Now make it a frosty, sunny October morning with 30% chlorophyll. How much anthocyanin do you need to remove the excess?
  2. Make it a warm, cloudy October day. Does anthocyanin still help? So should cloudy, mild autumns be more or less red?
  3. The model says every tree should go red on cold, sunny days. They don't. What has it left out?
Predict first Researchers took mutant shrubs that cannot make anthocyanin, and normal plants of the same species, and gave them five cold, bright days in autumn (Hoch et al. 2003). How much nitrogen did the mutants recover from their leaves compared with normal plants?
Reveal: the sunscreen works, with a twist

The mutants recovered less nitrogen, showed light damage that did not recover, and dropped their leaves while still green. Normal plants recovered.

The twist: paper birch, which makes no anthocyanin at all, recovered as much nitrogen as the best red species. Red is one solution, not the only one.

Stage C · 3 min · Through an aphid's eyes
Predict first Aphids have no receptor for red light. Can they tell a red autumn leaf from a green one?
Reveal

Researchers measured the light reflected by hundreds of real autumn leaves and worked out how each would look to an aphid. Red leaves were much less attractive than green ones, and yellow leaves much more attractive (Döring et al. 2009). Aphids don't need a red receptor: a red leaf simply lacks the yellow-green glow they are drawn to. Go back to the leaf mixer in section 1 and watch the aphid bar as you move from yellow to red.

In the field, one aphid species preferred green over red bird-cherry trees (Archetti & Leather 2005). Others argue this needs no coevolution at all: aphids simply like yellow-green (Chittka & Döring 2007).

From your essayA common argument in the essays was "aphids can't see red, so the insect idea is unlikely". Does that argument still work?
Stage D · If time Weigh the evidence

For each finding, decide which way it points. Several are arguable. That is the point.

Stage E · 4 min · Follow the nitrogen

Most essays treated the two ideas as rivals. Look at what each is about. Both concern the same nitrogen at the same moment: the amino acids flowing out of an ageing leaf through the phloem.

Why aphids care

Aphids feed on phloem sap. While a leaf is exporting, its sap is rich in amino acids: in one plant, about two and a half times richer than in a mature leaf (Merritt 1996). Aphids seek out the first yellowing leaves; on young birch trees they were found on 41% of yellow leaves but only 19% of green ones (Holopainen et al. 2009). Yellow may be a signpost to food (Holopainen & Peltonen 2002).

Why the tree cares

Every amino acid an aphid takes is nitrogen the tree does not store for spring. Aphids also lay eggs on the twigs, and their young feed on next spring's new leaves. Red that hides the yellow signpost, or warns insects off, protects the same nitrogen that the sunscreen protects.

A nitrogen ledger

One leaf holds 100 units of nitrogen. Too much light damages the export unless anthocyanin shades it. Aphids are drawn in by how yellow the leaf looks and take a share of what is exported.

Discuss
  1. Set the aphids to zero. Does anthocyanin still help the tree? Now set light stress to zero instead. Which idea is each of these settings?
  2. If nitrogen is what matters, which trees should be reddest? Does that fit the sugar maple result from Stage A?
  3. One group of researchers says autumn colour attracts aphids to food; another found red leaves unattractive. Can both be right if yellow and red mean different things?
My reading

Recovering nitrogen is the link. The sunscreen idea is about how much nitrogen the tree gets out of the leaf; the insect idea is about how much of it the tree keeps. Both predict that red should be strongest where nitrogen is most precious, such as nutrient-poor trees and short seasons. They still differ on who red is for: a sunscreen works whether or not anyone is watching, but a signal only works if aphids respond to it. So the test is a tree with lots of light stress but no aphids, or the reverse. That idea comes back in the next section.

Minutes 32–52 · Problem-based case

4 · Case: why is New England red and Oxford yellow?

Anyone who has seen a New England or Japanese autumn knows Europe is different. Most of our native trees go yellow or brown. Section 3 was about what happens in one leaf, this year. This case asks whether the answer lies thousands, or millions, of years back.

Stage A · 3 min · Count the red trees
Predict first Researchers took a list of 290 tree species with red autumn leaves and checked where each one grows (Lev-Yadun & Holopainen 2009). Roughly how many are native to Europe?
Reveal: the numbers
TogetherBefore you explain the pattern, question the numbers. The moist temperate forests of East Asia have about six times as many tree species as Europe's, and eastern North America about twice as many (Latham & Ricklefs 1993). Is Europe short of red trees, or just short of trees? What would you need to calculate to tell?
Quick review · ice ages and timescales
  • For tens of millions of years before the ice ages, the climate was warmer and very similar trees grew right across Europe, Asia and North America. Geologists used to call this period the Tertiary.
  • In the last 2.6 million years (the Pleistocene) ice sheets advanced and retreated dozens of times. Each cold period lasted tens of thousands of years.
  • Trees cannot walk, but their populations can shift as seeds establish further south. In each cold period, temperate trees survived only in warmer pockets called refugia, then spread back north. Species that could not reach a refugium died out in that region.
Stage B · 4 min · Three continents in the Ice Age
Discuss
  1. Follow a warmth-loving tree southwards on each map as the ice advances. Where does it end up, and what stands in its way?
  2. One suggestion is that in Europe both trees and the insects that fed on them were trapped between ice and the Alps and died out (Lev-Yadun & Holopainen 2009). If red is a message to insects, what happens to it in a tree whose insects are gone? Use the nitrogen ledger: set the aphids to zero.
Stage C · 5 min · The glacial squeeze

Three continents start with the same set of 60 tree lineages. Some can cope with cold, some need warmth. In each glacial cycle, a lineage survives only if it can reach somewhere warm enough. You decide which lineages are red to begin with.

Which lineages are red at the start?
Europe's east–west mountains
Discuss
  1. In mode A, does Europe end with fewer red lineages? Does it end with a lower percentage of red? Which of the two does the real pattern need?
  2. Now try mode B. What would have to be true about real trees for mode B to be right?
  3. Switch Europe's mountains off. Does Europe still lose most of its lineages? What else is stopping trees from retreating far enough south?
Push further: what if red only lasts while the insects do?

Mode C starts with red at random, but a surviving red lineage loses its colour if its insects died out; the harsher the squeeze on a continent, the likelier that is. This is the Lev-Yadun & Holopainen idea.

Stage D · If time Did red come from the tropics?

Here is a more speculative idea, and it is my own. Red leaves are common in the tropics, mostly in young leaves rather than dying ones. The ancestors of today's temperate trees lived in warmer climates. In each ice age, North American and East Asian trees could retreat towards warm, subtropical forests; European trees were trapped. Could temperate trees have kept red where they stayed close to warm, bright, insect-rich forests, and lost it where they didn't?

For

In a survey of 399 tropical plants, nearly half made anthocyanin in young leaves, and about 13% in dying leaves. That 13% is close to the 12% of temperate trees with red autumn leaves (Lee & Collins 2001; Archetti 2009), which suggests red autumn leaves are an old trait.

On one tropical island in Panama, over a third of tree species flush red young leaves (Kursar & Coley 1992).

Against, or complicating

A recent survey found red or pale young leaves just as often far from the tropics as near them (Chiarenza & Moles 2025).

Many European trees that go yellow in autumn have red young leaves in spring (Lev-Yadun 2022). So they can make anthocyanin; they just don't in autumn.

Discuss
  1. If a tree species "picked up" red in a refugium, what actually happened? Did individual trees change, did natural selection favour red-leaved variants that were already there, or did red-leaved species simply survive while others died out? Which is most likely in an ice age lasting tens of thousands of years?
  2. Red in young spring leaves and red in dying autumn leaves are often made in different cell layers. Is that one trait or two?
Stage E · If time The rival: today's weather

Another explanation needs no history at all (Renner & Zohner 2019). Eastern North America and East Asia get more sunshine than Europe. Eastern North American autumns also swing between warm days and sudden cold snaps, and the growing season there is about three weeks shorter. Bright sun plus cold snaps is exactly when the sunscreen should pay off most.

DiscussGo back to the light model and set a bright, cold day. Could history and today's weather both be true at once?
Stage F · 3 min · Design the test
TogetherYou have an arboretum (a garden of trees from around the world) with related species from Europe, North America and East Asia growing side by side. You can measure them for one autumn. For each explanation, what result would support it?
  1. The insects died out in Europe, so red was lost.
  2. The warmth-loving, red-leaved lineages died out in Europe.
  3. Today's sunnier, colder autumns make American trees red.
My reading
ExplanationWhat would support it
Insects died outEuropean species can still make anthocyanin (for example in young leaves) but don't in autumn, and have fewer specialist aphids than their American or Asian relatives.
Red lineages died outRed is concentrated in particular families with warm-climate relatives, and fossils show those families once grew in Europe.
Today's weatherEuropean species go red when grown in a sunny, cold-snap climate, and American species go less red in a cloudy, mild one.
