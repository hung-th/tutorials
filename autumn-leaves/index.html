Why are leaves sometimes red and falling?

A few weeks of autumn colour hide three separate questions. How does a leaf change colour and come off the tree? What does the tree gain from doing it? And why do some floras turn red while others go quietly yellow? This hour takes them in that order, and finishes with the Ice Ages.

How this hour works

Three questions, one leaf

Mechanism, function, history. Every section comes back to the leaves you brought. By the end you should be able to say which kind of answer each paper gives.

Predict before you look

Most results are hidden behind a reveal. Commit to a prediction first, out loud or in writing.

Play with the models

The interactive models are deliberately simple toys. They are there to argue with. If one gives an answer you think is wrong, say why.

Bring your essay

Where you see From your essay, find the paragraph where you made that claim, or should have, and be ready to defend or revise it.

By the end you should be able to

  • Trace the sequence from environmental cue to abscission, and say which steps are coupled and which can be separated.
  • Explain why yellow needs little explaining but red needs a lot.
  • Weigh the evidence for photoprotection and for signalling to insects, show how nutrient resorption links the two, and design an experiment that would separate them.
  • Explain why trees with red autumn leaves are commoner in eastern North America and East Asia than in Europe, using glacial history and at least one rival explanation.

No preparation needed

Everything you need is on these pages. Bring your essay and the leaves you collected.

Minutes 06–18

2 · Mechanism: from cue to fall

Opening questionA leaf goes yellow, then red, then falls. Is that one programme, or three that happen to run in sequence? What evidence would tell you?
Stage A · 4 min · What starts it?
Predict first Mature aspens in northern Sweden start autumn senescence on almost the same date every year. What is the trigger?
Reveal: three aspen studies, one tree species
StudyDesignConclusion
Keskitalo et al. 2005One free-growing tree, followed through autumn 2003Senescence started on 11 September, apparently triggered by photoperiod alone, and then ran steadily regardless of weather.
Fracheboud et al. 2009Natural stands plus cloned trees under different light regimesShortening photoperiod is the main trigger, but onset varied a lot within and between populations (tracking latitude of origin). Senescence only began some time after bud set, and low temperature sped it up once started.
Michelson et al. 2018116 genotypes from 12 Swedish populations in a northern and a southern common gardenThe timing was incompatible with day length per se. The trigger must be some other light-dependent factor.

Across many species, temperature and productivity matter too. Zani et al. (2020) argued that higher spring and summer productivity brings senescence forward (a sink-limitation effect); Lu & Keenan (2022) found no evidence for it in their data. Gill et al. (2015) review the trends.

From your essaySeveral essays said senescence is triggered "solely" by photoperiod. Which of these papers would you cite for that, and with what qualification? Why might one tree in one year give a cleaner answer than 116 genotypes in two gardens?
Stage B · 4 min · A cellular timetable
Discuss
  1. Once half the chlorophyll is gone, mitochondrial respiration powers remobilisation. Why not photosynthesis?
  2. Remobilisation stops around 29 September but the last leaves fall over a week later. What is the tree doing in that gap, and what would happen if a leaf fell on 20 September?
  3. About 80% of nitrogen and phosphorus left the leaves. Where does most of that nitrogen come from, if not chlorophyll?
Where the nitrogen is

Most leaf nitrogen is in protein, above all Rubisco and the light-harvesting complexes. Chlorophyll carries little of it, and its catabolites are not exported but stored in the vacuole. Breaking chlorophyll down is mainly about making the leaf safe while the proteins are dismantled: free chlorophyll is phototoxic (Hörtensteiner 2006).

Stage C · 4 min · How a leaf lets go
Discuss
  1. Young oaks and beech hedges keep dead brown leaves all winter (marcescence). What does that tell you about whether senescence and abscission are one programme?
  2. In Hoch et al. (2003), anthocyanin-deficient mutants put under bright, cold conditions dropped their leaves while still green. What does that suggest about the link between colour and fall?
From your essayMost essays gave falling a sentence or two at the end. If you had 300 more words, what would they say?

Minutes 18–32

3 · Function: what is red for?

Opening questionYellow is mostly what is left behind. Red is made, days before the leaf is thrown away. Why pay for a pigment at the end of a leaf's life?
Stage A · 2 min · Make the hypotheses predict
HypothesisCore ideaWho benefits?Which trees should be reddest?
Photoprotection (resorption protection)Anthocyanin screens the failing photosynthetic machinery, so nutrient recovery can finish (Hoch et al. 2001, 2003; Feild et al. 2001)you fill inyou fill in
Signalling to insectsRed is a costly, honest signal of defence to aphids that lay overwintering eggs (Archetti 2000; Hamilton & Brown 2001)you fill inyou fill in
Masking yellowRed hides the yellow that aphids find very attractive (Döring et al. 2009)you fill inyou fill in
My reading

Photoprotection: the tree itself; reddest should be leaves and trees that need resorption most and face most light stress, such as cold, bright days and nitrogen-poor trees. Signalling (honest-vigour version): tree and insect both; the reddest should be the best defended, often the most vigorous trees. Masking: the tree; redness should track how attractive the underlying yellow would be. Note that the first and second make opposite predictions about nutrient-poor trees. That is where Schaberg et al. (2003) comes in.

Stage B · 3 min · Light budget of a dying leaf

A toy model. Light absorbed by the chloroplasts is either used for carbon fixation, dissipated safely as heat (non-photochemical quenching), or left over. The leftover is what makes reactive oxygen species. As chloroplasts are dismantled, both safe routes shrink. Anthocyanin in the vacuole takes some light before it reaches the chloroplasts.

Discuss
  1. Find a summer setting with no excess. Now make it a frosty, sunny October morning with 30% chlorophyll. How much anthocyanin removes the excess?
  2. Make it a warm, cloudy October day. Does anthocyanin matter? What does the model predict about red colour in cloudy, mild autumns?
  3. The model says every tree should go red on cold, bright days. They don't. What has it left out?
Predict first Hoch et al. (2003) used anthocyanin-deficient mutants of three red-leaved species (Cornus sericea, Vaccinium elliottii, Viburnum sargentii) and gave them five days of high light and low temperature during senescence. Compared with wild type, how well did the mutants recover nitrogen?
Reveal: resorption protection, and a twist

The mutants had lower nitrogen resorption efficiency in both stressful treatments, lost photochemical efficiency and showed irreversible photo-oxidative damage, and dropped their leaves while still green. Wild type recovered.

The twist: paper birch (Betula papyrifera), which makes no anthocyanin in autumn, resorbed nitrogen as well as the best of the red species. Red is one solution, not the only one.

Stage C · 3 min · Through an aphid's eyes
Predict first Aphids have no red photoreceptor. Can they tell a red autumn leaf from a green one?
Reveal: Döring, Archetti & Hardie (2009)

Using reflectance spectra of real autumn leaves and a model of aphid colour choice, leaves that look red to us were on average much less attractive to aphids than green leaves, and yellow leaves were much more attractive. Their conclusion: active avoidance of red is unlikely, but red could still signal tree quality, or it could serve to mask the over-attractive yellow left when chlorophyll is recovered. Go back to the leaf mixer in section 1 and watch the aphid index as you move from yellow to red.

Field tests: Rhopalosiphum padi on bird cherry (Prunus padus) preferred green over red trees (Archetti & Leather 2005). Chittka & Döring (2007) argued that this kind of preference needs no coevolution to explain it.

From your essayA common argument in the essays was "aphids can't see red, so signalling is unlikely". What does Döring et al. (2009) do to that argument, and does it rescue the signalling hypothesis or not?
Stage D · 3 min · Weigh the evidence

For each finding, decide which way it points. Several are arguable. That is the point.

Stage E · 3 min · Follow the nitrogen

Most essays treated photoprotection and coevolution as rivals. Look at what each one is actually about. Both are about the same pool of nitrogen, at the same moment: the amino acids streaming out of a senescing leaf through the phloem.

Why aphids care about resorption

During export, leaf nitrogen is in the phloem as free amino acids, which is exactly what aphids feed on. In black mustard, phloem sap from senescing leaves carried about 199 mM amino acids, against 77–83 mM in mature leaves (Merritt 1996). Holopainen & Peltonen (2002) proposed that autumn colour works as a cue to this food: aphids seek out the first yellowing leaves. In birch seedlings, aphids were found on 41% of yellow leaves but only 19% of green ones, driven by the winged Euceraphis betulae (Holopainen et al. 2009).

Why the tree cares about aphids

Every amino acid an aphid takes is nitrogen the tree does not store for spring. Aphids also lay overwintering eggs on twigs, so the colonists of this autumn feed on next spring's flush. Red that masks the "nitrogen here" yellow (Döring et al. 2009), or that honestly advertises defence (Archetti 2000; Hamilton & Brown 2001), protects the same nitrogen that anthocyanin's light screen protects.

A nitrogen ledger

A toy model of one leaf's 100 units of nitrogen. Light stress damages the export machinery unless anthocyanin screens it. Aphids are drawn in by how yellow the leaf looks and take a share of whatever is exported.

Discuss
  1. Set aphid pressure to zero. How much does anthocyanin gain the tree now? Set light stress to zero instead. Each "rival" hypothesis is a special case of the ledger. Which one?
  2. If the currency is nitrogen, which trees should be reddest? Check your answer against Schaberg et al. (2003): nitrogen-poor sugar maples went red earlier and more fully.
  3. Holopainen & Peltonen argued bright colour attracts aphids as an honest cue of food; Döring et al. found red leaves unattractive. Can both be right if yellow and red mean different things?
  4. Write one plausible history in which red first evolved for photoprotection and was later shaped by aphids, or the other way round. What evidence would tell the two orders apart?
My reading

Resorption is the common currency. Photoprotection asks how much nitrogen the tree can get out of the leaf; coevolution asks how much of that it keeps. Both predict that red should be strongest where nitrogen is most valuable (nitrogen-poor trees, short seasons) and during the export window rather than before or after it, which is what Keskitalo et al. (2005) and Schaberg et al. (2003) found. Where the two still part company is who red is for: a light screen works whether or not anyone is watching; a signal or mask only works if aphids respond to it. So the test is a tree with high light stress but no aphids, or the reverse, and island or introduced populations are natural experiments of exactly that kind. That idea comes back in the biogeography case.

Minutes 32–52 · Problem-based case

4 · Case: why is New England red and Oxford yellow?

Anyone who has seen a Vermont or Kyoto autumn knows Europe is different. Most of our native trees go yellow or brown. Both hypotheses from section 3 are about what happens in one leaf, this year. This case asks whether the answer lies thousands, or millions, of years back.

Stage A · 3 min · Count the red trees
Predict first Archetti (2009) compiled a list of 290 tree species with red autumn leaves. Lev-Yadun & Holopainen (2009) checked where each one grows. Roughly how many are native to Europe?
Reveal: the numbers
TogetherBefore you explain the pattern, attack the numbers. Mesic temperate forests in East Asia hold roughly three times as many tree species as eastern North America, and six times as many as Europe (Latham & Ricklefs 1993). Is Europe short of red trees, or just short of trees? What number would you need to tell?
Stage B · 4 min · Three continents in the Ice Age
Discuss
  1. Follow a warmth-loving tree southwards on each map as the ice advances. Where does it end up, and what stands in its way?
  2. Lev-Yadun & Holopainen (2009) argue that in Europe the biota was trapped between northern ice and the Alps, so more trees and their specialist insects went extinct. If the coevolution hypothesis is right, what happens to red colour in a tree whose aphids are gone? Use the nitrogen ledger: what is red still worth if only light stress is left?
  3. Bemmels et al. (2019) found genomic evidence that one North American hickory survived close to the ice margin, while another retreated far south. Does one map fit every species?
Stage C · 5 min · The glacial squeeze

Three continents start with the same Tertiary tree flora: 60 lineages, each with its own warmth requirement. Each glacial cycle pushes the climate south. A lineage survives a cycle if it can reach somewhere warm enough. You choose how redness is linked to everything else.

How is red distributed in the starting flora?
Europe's east–west mountain barrier
Discuss
  1. In mode A, does Europe end with fewer red lineages? With a lower proportion of red? Which of those does the real pattern need?
  2. Which modes reproduce both fewer and proportionally fewer red trees in Europe? What would you have to believe about real trees for each mode to be true?
  3. Switch the mountain barrier off. Svenning (2003) found that the European losses were predictable from climate tolerance: genera that went extinct were less tolerant of cold growing seasons and winters than those that survived. Is a mountain barrier needed at all?
Stage D · 4 min · Did red come from the tropics?

Here is a more speculative idea, and it is my own. Red leaves are common in the tropics, mostly in young leaves rather than dying ones. Temperate deciduous lineages descend from warmer, often evergreen, ancestors. During glacials, eastern North American and East Asian trees retreated towards warm-temperate and subtropical latitudes; European trees were pinned against mountains and sea. Could temperate trees have kept, or picked up, red where they stayed in contact with warm, bright, herbivore-rich floras?

For

In 399 tropical species, 44.9% made anthocyanin in young leaves and 13.5% in senescing leaves (Lee & Collins 2001). That 13.5% is close to the 12.2% of temperate species with red autumn leaves in Archetti's survey, which Lev-Yadun & Holopainen (2009) read as a sign that red autumn leaves are old, a Tertiary relict.

36% of tree species on Barro Colorado Island flush red young leaves (Kursar & Coley 1992).

Against, or complicating

Across 105 species from 16°S to 43°S, delayed greening showed no latitudinal gradient (Chiarenza & Moles 2025). Red young leaves are not just a tropical thing.

In Viburnum, deciduousness evolved in place, in parallel, as climate cooled (Edwards et al. 2017), and most deciduous lineages took up leaf-shedding only after reaching freezing climates (Zanne et al. 2014). Many European yellow-autumn species have red young leaves anyway (Lev-Yadun 2022), so the capacity is there.

Discuss
  1. What could "picking up red" in a refugium actually mean? New mutation, selection on existing variation, hybridisation with subtropical relatives, or simply which lineages survived? Which is plausible over ~100,000-year glacial cycles?
  2. Red in young tropical leaves and red in dying temperate leaves use the same pathway, but often in different tissues: epidermis in spring, palisade in autumn, in Corylus and Acer platanoides (Merzlyak et al. 2008). Same trait or different trait?
  3. Genera that occur in both East Asia and eastern North America but not in Europe are classic Tertiary relicts (Wen 1999; Milne & Abbott 2002). How would you use them to test this idea?
Stage E · 2 min · The rival: today's climate

Renner & Zohner (2019) offer a present-day explanation. Eastern North America and East Asia receive more solar irradiance than Europe. Eastern North America also has larger temperature swings in autumn, so cold snaps hit during senescence, and its species have on average a 3-week shorter vegetation period. In a common garden, chlorophyll breakdown started earlier in eastern North American species than in their European and East Asian relatives. Bright light plus cold snaps is exactly when anthocyanin photoprotection should pay off most.

DiscussGo back to the light-budget model and set a bright, cold day. Then ask: are the history explanation and the climate explanation alternatives, or could both be true? What does a common-garden result tell you that a field survey cannot?
Stage F · 2 min · Design the test
TogetherYou have an arboretum with congeneric species from Europe, eastern North America and East Asia, a dated phylogeny, and a budget for one autumn of measurements. Write one prediction that would distinguish each explanation:
  1. Herbivore co-extinction (Lev-Yadun & Holopainen 2009)
  2. Climatic filtering of warm-origin lineages (Svenning 2003, plus a tropical origin of red)
  3. Present-day light and cold snaps (Renner & Zohner 2019)
My reading
ExplanationPrediction
Herbivore co-extinctionEuropean species should still carry, and be able to express, the anthocyanin machinery but have lost the autumn trigger. Their specialist aphid fauna should be poorer than their American or Asian congeners'. Redness should track aphid diversity within genera (as Hamilton & Brown 2001 found across species).
Climatic filtering of warm-origin lineagesRed should be phylogenetically clustered in lineages with warm, subtropical affinity. In Europe, the lineages that went extinct (from the fossil record) should be disproportionately red-leaved relatives. Redness should not depend on where the tree grows today.
Present-day light and coldIn a common garden, European species should go red when given bright, cold autumns. Within species, populations from sunnier, more variable climates should be redder. Redness should respond to experimental light and temperature.
