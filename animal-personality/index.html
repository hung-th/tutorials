Is it really what's inside that counts?

Your essays argued about whether animals "have" personalities, what builds them and why selection keeps them around. In this hour we test those arguments against one real dataset, and against each other.

How this hour works

Questions, not answers

Each section opens with a question. Most have no single right answer. I will push back on whatever you say, including when you are right.

Bring your essay

Where you see From your essay, find the paragraph where you made that claim and be ready to defend or revise it.

Predict before you look

In the case study, write down your prediction before opening each reveal. Being wrong in writing is how you learn the most here.

Play with the models

The sliders are there to argue with. If a model tells you something you think is wrong, say why.

By the end you should be able to

  1. Separate personality, plasticity and behavioural syndromes, and say what data you would need to show each.
  2. Explain what a repeatability of 0.24 does and does not tell you.
  3. Critically evaluate a candidate-gene study of behaviour: design, statistics and what it can claim.
  4. Weigh the main explanations for why individual differences persist under selection.

No preparation needed

Everything you need is on these pages, including the parts of the research paper we discuss. Come with your essay and an open mind.

Minutes 00–10

1 · What counts as personality?

First minuteWrite your current answer to the essay question in one sentence. Keep it. We come back to it in the last three minutes.
Warm-up · 2 min · predict first

Amazon mollies reproduce clonally, so sisters are genetically identical. Researchers separated newborn sisters and raised each alone in identical tanks, with identical food and light, then tracked their activity for weeks. What do you expect?

Reveal: what happened

Consistent individual differences emerged anyway (Bierbach, Laskowski & Wolf 2017). Same genes and, as far as anyone could control it, the same environment. So where did the differences come from, and is that "inside"? Hold that thought until the wrap-up.

Opening questionYour neighbour's dog is "friendly". What would you need to measure before a behavioural ecologist would accept that as a personality trait?

Sort these

For each observation, decide which concept it is evidence for. Discuss disagreements; several are deliberately ambiguous.

Personality and plasticity at the same time

A common worry in your essays: if animals adjust behaviour to circumstances, how can behaviour also be a fixed "personality"? Below, each line is one individual's boldness across a gradient of predation risk (a behavioural reaction norm). The intercept is its average level; the slope is its plasticity.

Discuss
  1. Set the mean slope steeply negative and the spread of slopes to zero. Is every bird plastic? Do they still have personalities?
  2. Now increase the spread of slopes until lines cross. What has happened to "personality"? Is "how plastic you are" itself a personality trait?
  3. From your essay Several of you set personality and plasticity against each other. Does this picture change your argument?

Minutes 10–20

2 · Measuring it: repeatability and validity

Opening questionA meta-analysis found the average repeatability of behaviour is about 0.37 (Bell et al. 2009). In plain English, what does that number mean? What does it not mean?

Repeatability is a ratio of variances

R = Vamong individuals / (Vamong individuals + Vwithin individuals)

Discuss
  1. How much does the estimate jump around at n = 20? What does this mean for a single published R value?
  2. If you raise the within-individual variance, R falls. Is that because animals are "less consistent", or could your assay be noisy? How would you tell?
  3. Does a high R prove the behaviour is genetic?

Does your assay measure what you think it measures?

Santos et al. (2015) gave homing pigeons three tests and then tracked them while raptors hunted them on homing flights.

Predict first (1 min)

For each test, which birds do you think were more likely to be killed? Commit before you open the reveal.

A nudge if you're stuck

Look for "construct validity" and "convergent validity". A trait that is defined only by the test used to measure it is hard to compare across studies. Keep this in mind: the case study uses yet another boldness assay.

Minutes 20–42 · Problem-based case

3 · Genes, boldness and stress in homing pigeons

You work through a real study in five stages. At each stage you decide something before seeing what the authors did. The study is Ramadan, Hung, Delacoux, Kano, Inoue-Murayama & Biro (2026), Physiology & Behavior 305: 115211, open access.

The paper in two minutes

Ramadan, Hung, Delacoux, Kano, Inoue-Murayama & Biro (2026). Associations of genetic polymorphisms with boldness, stress response, and route efficiency in homing pigeons (Columba livia). Physiology & Behavior 305: 115211.

Question. Do common variants in genes for dopamine signalling, serotonin synthesis and energy metabolism go with consistent differences in boldness and stress response?

Birds. 137 homing pigeons (75 male, 60 female, 2 unknown), aged 1–20 years, at the John Krebs Field Station, Wytham. An avian influenza prevention zone kept them indoors throughout.

What was measured. Three genes genotyped from feathers; a shelter-emergence test of boldness, repeated 36 days apart; eye temperature by thermal camera during 10 minutes of social isolation, with and without a mirror; and, for 20 birds, route efficiency from earlier GPS flights.

You'll design the study yourselves first. Each stage below then reveals what was actually done and found.

Your role for the next 20 minutes

Geneticist

Do the genotype–behaviour claims hold up? Sample size, number of tests, what the variants actually change.

Behavioural ecologist

Does the emergence test measure boldness? Is it repeatable enough to call personality?

Physiologist

What does eye temperature tell us about stress? What is a mirror, to a pigeon?

Reviewer 2

Find the weakest link in the whole argument, then propose the experiment that would fix it.

At each Discuss box, whoever's role fits best speaks first. With fewer than four of you, double up.

Stage A · 5 min · Design it yourself

The brief

You have 137 homing pigeons at a field station near Oxford, aged 1 to 20 years. Bird flu restrictions mean they cannot fly outdoors during your study. You can collect feathers for DNA, you have a thermal camera, and older GPS tracks exist for a few of the birds. Your question: do individual differences in boldness and stress response have a genetic basis?

In pairs
  1. Which genes would you look at first, and why those?
  2. How would you measure boldness in a bird that cannot leave the loft? How many times per bird?
  3. How could you measure a stress response without catching and bleeding the bird?
Reveal: what the authors chose

Boldness

Emergence test: bird placed in a cardboard shelter, 3 min to settle, then time to walk out into a 140 cm passage. Cut-off 600 s. Every bird tested twice, 36 days apart.

Stress response

Ten minutes alone in a novel two-compartment cage, maximum eye temperature filmed every minute by thermal camera. Repeated with a mirror in the empty compartment as a social cue. 134 birds did both.

Navigation

Route efficiency (straight-line ÷ actual distance) from earlier GPS releases 5.2 km from home. Only 20 genotyped birds had tracks.

DiscussWhy might "a coding SNP that doesn't change the protein" still be associated with behaviour? List at least two ways. And the elephant in the room: why choose three candidate genes rather than scan the whole genome?
Stage B · 5 min · Is it personality at all?

Before linking genes to boldness, the authors had to check that emergence was repeatable.

PredictMany birds never came out within 10 minutes. How would that affect repeatability? Write down a number you would expect for R.
Reveal: repeatability

Whether a bird emerged within 10 min was repeatable across the two trials: R = 0.235 (p = 0.004, n = 137). Latency among birds that emerged was not significantly repeatable (p = 0.146, n = 74).

Discuss
  1. Is 0.235 enough to call this a personality trait? Go back to the simulator in section 2: what range would you expect with n = 137?
  2. Why might whether a bird emerges be repeatable but how fast it emerges not be?
  3. Thirty-six days between trials, birds aged 1–20 years, no outdoor flying. What might change inside a bird between trials?
Stage C · 7 min · Genes and boldness
PredictFor each gene, sketch which genotype you expect to be boldest. Commit to it.
Reveal: mean emergence latency by genotype
Discuss
  1. Why a Cox survival model instead of comparing mean latencies with an ANOVA? (Think about the birds that scored 600.)
  2. DRD4 looks additive: each T allele makes birds bolder. TPH2 heterozygotes are boldest. What genetic explanations fit that pattern, and what boring explanation fits it too?
  3. Two p-values just under 0.05, across three genes and several traits. How worried should we be? What would make you more confident?
  4. Suppose the DRD4 effect is real. How much of the variation in boldness could one SNP possibly explain? Compare with the heritability of personality, roughly half of the repeatable variation (Dochtermann et al. 2015).
Stage D · 5 min · Stress, mirrors and context

Eye temperature was filmed each minute while a bird sat alone in a novel cage, once with an empty compartment next to it and once with a mirror there.

PredictWill the mirror calm the birds (a "companion") or stress them more (a "stranger")? Why might eye temperature change at all when a bird is stressed?
Reveal: what happened
Discuss
  1. With a mirror, eye temperature climbed back faster in every genotype, which the authors read as social buffering. Is a mirror a social cue, a novel object or a rival? Does the answer matter for how you interpret LDHA?
  2. Notice that DRD4 and TPH2 are linked to both boldness and the stress response. Is that evidence for a behavioural syndrome, a shared mechanism, or neither?
  3. LDHA is a metabolic gene. If it shapes how birds respond to social context, what does that say about where "personality" lives in the body? Link this to the pace-of-life idea in section 4.
Stage E · 4 min · Be the reviewer

Route efficiency showed no genotype effect, but only 20 birds had GPS data and the authors call it inconclusive.

In pairsYou are reviewing this paper. Write the one extra analysis or experiment you would ask for, and the one claim you would ask the authors to soften. Then swap with the other pair and argue.
Some threads to pull on
  • Candidate genes vs genome-wide approaches; replication in another population.
  • Multiple testing across genes and traits.
  • No resting baseline after habituation: what does "stress recovery" mean here?
  • Captive birds under lockdown: would wild or free-flying birds behave the same?
  • Can an association ever show that a gene causes boldness? What would?
History lesson · Geneticist leadsA DRD4 variant was linked to exploration in great tits (Fidler et al. 2007). When it was tested across four wild populations, the association did not replicate consistently (Korsten et al. 2010). Give three reasons a real gene–behaviour association might vanish in a new population, and three reasons a false one might appear in the first place.

Minutes 42–52

4 · Why does variation persist?

Opening questionIf DRD4 T/T birds really are bolder, and boldness affects fitness, why hasn't selection fixed one allele already?

One answer: frequency dependence

In the hawk-dove game, hawks always fight and doves never do. A contested resource is worth V; losing a fight costs C. Watch how the proportion of hawks changes over generations.

Discuss
  1. The model reaches the same mix whether every bird plays hawk 40% of the time or 40% of birds always play hawk. Which version is "personality"? Can the model tell them apart?
  2. What would have to be true about pigeons for DRD4 genotypes to be maintained this way?

The other candidates

Life-history trade-offs

Animals with more to lose in the future should take fewer risks now, and that difference should apply across contexts (Wolf et al. 2007).

Pigeon link: birds aged 1 to 20 years. Did older birds behave differently?

Pace-of-life syndrome

Fast metabolism, fast life history and bold behaviour should travel together. A large meta-analysis found surprisingly weak support (Royauté et al. 2018).

Pigeon link: LDHA is a metabolic gene.

Social niche specialisation

In groups, individuals do better by being different from each other, which reinforces differences (Bergmüller & Taborsky 2010).

Pigeon link: bold pigeons lead flocks (Sasaki et al. 2018).

Fluctuating selection

Bold wins in some years or places, shy in others, so neither is fixed.

Pigeon link: raptors favour some "bold" behaviours and punish others (Santos et al. 2015).

Quick debate (4 min)Each of you takes one mechanism above and makes the case that it best explains individual differences in homing pigeons. The others must name the evidence that would prove you wrong.

Minutes 52–57

5 · Does it matter outside the lab?

ScenarioA conservation programme breeds swift foxes in captivity for release. Bremner-Harrison et al. (2004) found the boldest captive-bred foxes were the most likely to die soon after release.
Discuss
  1. Should managers screen animals for personality before release? Which ones would you release, and what would you lose?
  2. If boldness is partly genetic (as in the pigeons), could captive breeding change allele frequencies at genes like DRD4? How quickly?
  3. From your essay One of you argued personality is "no more important than morphology". Does this example support or undermine that?
  4. Beadlet anemones, with no brain, show repeatable individual differences in startle recovery (Briffa & Greenaway 2011). Does that make "personality" a more useful word for animals, or a less useful one?
