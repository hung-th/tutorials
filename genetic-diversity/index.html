What determines the spatial distribution of genetic diversity?

We know the forces that change genetic diversity: mutation, drift, gene flow and selection. We are much worse at predicting where on a map diversity will be high or low. This hour asks why, at three scales: a whole species range, the whole planet, and a single tree nursery.

How this hour works

One question, three scales

Every section comes back to the title. By the end you should be able to say which force dominates at which scale, and why.

Predict before you look

Most results are hidden behind a reveal. Commit to a prediction first, out loud or in writing.

Play with the models

The simulators are there to argue with. If one gives an answer you think is wrong, say why.

Bring your essay

Where you see From your essay, find the paragraph where you made that claim and be ready to defend or revise it.

By the end you should be able to

  • Predict how drift and gene flow change diversity within and between populations, and say which measure reacts first.
  • State what the central–marginal, latitudinal and historical hypotheses predict, and the evidence for each.
  • Judge what a global dataset like GenDivRange can and cannot tell us.
  • Diagnose where genetic diversity is lost in a real restoration programme, and design a seed collection that avoids it.

No preparation needed

Everything you need is on these pages, including the parts of both case-study papers we discuss. Come with your essay and an open mind.

1 · Recap: four forces, two measures

First minuteWrite your answer to the essay question in one sentence. Keep it. We come back to it at the end.
Opening questionName the four forces that change allele frequencies. For each one, does it raise or lower diversity within a population? And between populations?
Check your table
ForceWithin a populationBetween populations
MutationUp (slowly)Up (new alleles arise in one place)
Genetic driftDown, faster when Ne is smallUp (populations drift apart)
Gene flowUp (brings in alleles)Down (homogenises)
SelectionEither: balancing keeps variants, directional removes themEither: divergent selection raises it, uniform selection lowers it

Drift against gene flow

Eight populations start identical, with 50 independent loci at p = 0.5. Each generation they drift, and a proportion m of each population is replaced by migrants from a common pool (an island model).

Discuss
  1. Set m = 0. Compare N = 20 and N = 300. What happens to diversity within populations, and to differentiation between them? Why do they move in opposite directions?
  2. Set N = 100 and slowly raise m. At roughly what m does FST stop climbing? Calculate Nm there. Theory says FST ≈ 1 / (1 + 4Nm): does the simulation agree?
  3. "One migrant per generation" is a famous rule of thumb. What does it protect, and what does it not?

Which measure?

Set allele frequencies at one locus and watch two measures respond: the number of alleles (A) and expected heterozygosity, HE = 1 − Σpi2.

Discuss
  1. Make two populations with the same number of alleles but very different HE. Then make two with the same HE but different numbers of alleles.
  2. A bottleneck removes the rarest allele. Which measure barely moves? Which one would you use to detect a recent bottleneck?
  3. From your essay One of you used "alpha" and "beta" diversity for within- and between-population variation. In population genetics we use HS, HT and FST. Why does the vocabulary matter?

Minutes 08–20

2 · Patterns: centre, latitude, history

Opening questionYou have only one fact about a population: where it sits on a map. What would you predict about its genetic diversity, and why?

The central–marginal hypothesis

Predict first

Eckert, Samis & Lougheed (2008) reviewed 134 studies of 115 species. In what proportion do you expect edge populations to have lower diversity? And how often were range-edge populations even less abundant (Sagarin & Gaines 2002, 145 tests)?

Reveal: the reviews

Lower diversity at the edge in 64% of studies that tested it; higher differentiation in 70% of the 57 that tested that (Eckert et al. 2008). Yet the abundant-centre pattern itself held in only 39% of 145 tests (Sagarin & Gaines 2002).

Puzzle: if edges are often not less abundant, why are they so often less diverse?

Latitude and history

Discuss
  1. De Kort et al. (2021) analysed 8,356 populations and found surprisingly weak latitudinal trends: none overall in plants, weak in animals, and the reverse in eudicots. Using the diagram, give two reasons a latitude effect could be hidden.
  2. Hirao et al. (2017) found the arctic–alpine Dryas octopetala had its lowest diversity at its southern edge. Where does that fit on the diagram?
  3. From your essay One of you supported the genetic latitudinal gradient with papers about species diversity. Why can't you assume the two gradients go together?

Pattern to process

Match each observed pattern to the process that best explains it. Several have more than one defensible answer.

Minutes 20–33 · Problem-based case 1

3 · Case: GenDivRange, the global view

Single-species studies disagree. Macrogenetics asks whether a pattern holds across hundreds of species at once. The case is Csilléry, Yang, Hung et al. (2025) Scientific Data 12: 980. Yes, I'm an author, so attack it freely.

Stage A · 3 min · Design it yourself

The brief

You want to test the central–marginal hypothesis across as many species as possible, using only data that has already been published.

Together
  1. What three pieces of information do you need for every population?
  2. Where would you get each species' range from, if most studies don't report it?
  3. Name the two biggest problems with pooling heterozygosity values from different studies.
Reveal: what GenDivRange did

1,109 species

Each with at least five genotyped populations.

19,173 populations

From 1,480 studies: terrestrial, freshwater and marine.

~90% microsatellites

Also allozymes, ISSR, RFLP and SNPs. HE, Nei's gene diversity or allelic richness for every study.

Ranges from GBIF

Cleaned occurrence records, gridded at 0.1° and 0.5°, so each population can be placed on its species' range.

How it was built

  • Sources: 287 newly collected studies, plus studies drawn from three earlier compilations (De Kort et al. 468, MacroPopGen 379, VarVer 346), merged and de-duplicated. The authors estimate over 3,000 published studies with at least five populations exist, so this is far from all of them.
  • For each population: latitude and longitude, sample size, marker type, and whichever of HE, HO, Nei's gene diversity, allelic richness, allele counts and FIS the study reported.
  • For each species: taxonomy, one of 14 life forms (mammals to mosses), biome, breeding and adult habitat (terrestrial, freshwater, marine) and IUCN status.
  • Ranges: GBIF records (plus OBIS for marine species) with duplicates, uncertain coordinates and zoo or garden records removed.
  • Checks: new entries checked by a second person; coordinates checked against each paper; automatic range checks (H between 0 and 1). The 39 studies where HO was implausibly far above HE were examined, and 30 turned out to contain typos.
  • Access: the data are on Figshare and the code on GitHub. A web app at gendivrange.org shows a map tab (each population coloured by HE, with a pop-up linking to the original paper) and a searchable species table, with linked filters for life form, marker, habitat, biome and species.
Stage B · 5 min · Explore the database

Hands on: gendivrange.org

We'll open the site together on screen during the tutorial. The map tab shows each population coloured by HE; the table tab lists species. Filters (life form, marker, habitat, biome) are linked between the two tabs.

  1. Pick a species with populations spread across its range. Before you look at the colours, predict where HE will be highest.
  2. Look. Does the pattern fit the central–marginal hypothesis? A latitudinal one? A history-based one?
  3. Filter by biome. Which biomes are dense with data, and which are almost empty?
  4. Open one population's pop-up and follow it to the original paper. How many individuals were genotyped, with which markers?
DiscussIf the two of you chose different species and got different patterns, is that a problem for the hypothesis, or for the data?
Stage C · 5 min · Where is the centre?

To test the central–marginal hypothesis you have to say how central each population is. That sounds trivial. Switch between three definitions and watch the ranking change.

Discuss
  1. Where does the centroid fall? What does that say about using it for real ranges, which are rarely blobs?
  2. Which population changes rank the most between definitions? If a paper reports "no support for the CMH", what should you ask first?
  3. Lira-Noriega & Manthey (2014) found diversity tracked niche centrality more consistently than geographic centrality. Why might that be?
Stage D · 5 min · Be the reviewer

Some facts from the paper's own validation and usage notes:

  • Temperate and Mediterranean biomes and wealthy regions are over-represented; tropical forests, especially in Africa, are under-studied.
  • About 90% of the data are microsatellites, whose mutation rates differ between loci and studies.
  • About 5.9% of populations had fewer than 10 diploid individuals genotyped.
  • Most studies did not correct for sample size.
TogetherWrite the one analysis you would never run on this dataset, and the one you think it is best placed to answer. Then argue for each.
DiscussThe data are biased towards species that are not threatened. If you used GenDivRange to set a global target for "healthy" genetic diversity, which way would the bias push your target?

Minutes 33–52 · Problem-based case 2

4 · Case: rebuilding Araucaria nemorosa

From the whole planet to one nursery. The same four forces operate, but here people control one of them. Data from Kettle et al. (2007, 2008), as presented in the Forest Genetic Resources Training Guide case study by Kettle & Boshier (2014).

Stage A · 3 min · The brief

Araucaria nemorosa is a Critically Endangered conifer known from only eight populations in the far south of New Caledonia, with an area of occupancy of 0.64 km². Fire, introduced mammals, logging and nickel mining have left under 30% of the original vegetation. Two nurseries are growing seedlings for restoration:

Kaanua (N1)

One of the largest populations, over 1,000 trees. The nursery collected cones from about 30 trees.

Forêt Nord (N6)

The smallest and only inland population, about 93 trees, inside a nickel processing site. The nursery gathered freshly fallen seed from the forest floor.

Species facts you will need

  • Long-lived, hermaphrodite tree with separate male and female cones on the same tree. No self-incompatibility, so it can self-pollinate.
  • Pollen is carried by wind; seed falls by gravity and wind, so most lands near the mother.
  • Female cones hold several hundred seeds. Seed germinates within days of release and cannot be stored (it is recalcitrant), so fallen seed must be collected promptly.
  • Grows on nickel-rich ultramafic soils. Remnant stands have many seedlings but few trees of 5–20 cm diameter, so recruitment into adults is poor.
  • It grows alongside dense stands of the widespread A. columnaris, which contributes heavily to the local pollen cloud.
Predict first

Which nursery stock will hold more of its source population's genetic diversity?

Discuss
  1. Kaanua has over 1,000 trees. How many of them do you think contribute seed in a given year? What is its effective population size likely to be, compared with its census size?
  2. Most seeds are empty. Give two biological explanations. (Hint: these trees are hermaphrodite and can self.)

Discuss
  1. Was your Stage A prediction right? How can 30 sampled trees give an effective number of parents of 1?
  2. The Kaanua nursery stock shows no inbreeding (FIS slightly negative). Why is that not good news here?
  3. The Forêt Nord nursery has plenty of diversity but the highest FIS of any cohort. Why would inbred seedlings survive in a nursery but not in the wild?
  4. Across all six populations, wild seedlings were more inbred than adults (mean FIS 0.192 vs 0.108). What does that tell you is happening between the seedling and adult stage?
Stage D · 4 min · Simulate the seed collection

A collector samples seed from several mother trees. Fecundity is very unequal: a few trees produce most of the viable seed. How many mothers are you really sampling?

Discuss
  1. With bulk pooling and high inequality, how much does doubling the number of mothers help? What helps more?
  2. Why did gathering fallen seed from the forest floor at Forêt Nord work better than picking cones from 30 trees at Kaanua? Was that luck or design?
Stage E · 3 min · Map the supply chain and fix it

Who is involved, and the constraints they work under

  • Institut Agronomique Néo-Calédonien: ran the Kaanua nursery, collecting cones from about 30 trees.
  • Vale Inco (nickel company): ran the Forêt Nord nursery at its Goro site, gathering fallen seed for post-mining revegetation.
  • Landowners: about 70% of the remaining stands (populations N1–N6) are on one family's land. The family is supportive and works well with the Environment Service.
  • Government: the Southern Province's Environment Service handles regulation and monitoring; CIRAD and IRD do forestry research and seed work.
  • Practicalities: collections happen when budgets and nursery space allow, not in the best cone years. Germination is only 30–40%. International donors rarely fund work in New Caledonia because it is part of France.
TogetherWrite a seed collection protocol for the next season, in five bullet points, specific enough that a nursery technician could follow it. Include how many trees, how much seed from each, which populations, and what you would record.
DiscussForêt Nord is the most genetically distinct population and sits inside a nickel plant. Should restoration mix seed from different populations, or keep them separate? What would you need to know to decide?

Minutes 52–60

Synthesis and exit question

Opening questionA nursery that takes most of its seed from one cone, and a species that recolonised northwards after the last glaciation, have something in common. What?

Same forces, three scales

For each cell, decide whether the force matters a lot, a little, or not at all at that scale. Be ready to defend your call.

Force or factorA nursery (one season)A species range (thousands of years)The globe (across species)

Tap a cell to cycle through: blank, a lot, a little, not at all.

Exit question (one sentence each)
  1. Re-read the sentence you wrote at the start. Rewrite it using one piece of evidence from today, or say why you would keep it.
  2. If you could add one variable to every population in GenDivRange, what would it be, and why?
  3. Name one place where human decisions now shape the spatial distribution of genetic diversity as strongly as glaciations once did.

Take-home puzzle

The Kaanua nursery lost most of its rare alleles in one season, yet its FST from the wild population was only about 0.16. Meanwhile populations that have been separated for thousands of years can have FST values well below that. What does that tell you about using FST to judge how "different" two populations really are?
